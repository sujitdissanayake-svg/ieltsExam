The Evolution of Lenses and Optics

When one mentions lenses, an image springs to most people’s minds of modern spectacles resting on a person’s nose, or the complex, multi-layered zoom lenses attached to contemporary digital cameras. Yet, thousands of years before the modern optical industry began manufacturing precise contact lenses and microscopic sensors, ancient civilisations were already experimenting with the manipulation of light. The history of optics is a long and complex journey, born out of a desire to magnify the microscopic and bring the distant cosmos into clearer view. The oldest known manufactured lens is the Nimrud lens, an ancient artifact dating back to the Assyrian empire in the 8th century BCE. Discovered in the ruins of the ancient city of Nimrud in modern-day Iraq, this slightly oval piece of polished rock crystal has a focal length of about 12 centimetres. While some historians argue it was merely a piece of decorative jewellery, others maintain it was used as a crude magnifying glass by craftsmen making intricate engravings.

The earliest lenses were not made from glass, but from naturally occurring transparent minerals. This was partly due to the great difficulty of producing perfectly clear glass in the ancient world, and partly due to the abundance of raw geological materials. Ancient artisans predominantly used rock crystal (pure quartz), a material that is highly transparent but incredibly hard. To shape these stones into functioning optical devices, craftsmen possessed unsurpassed patience and skill. They relied heavily on abrasive techniques, rubbing the crystal endlessly with a mixture of sand and water to slowly wear away the surface until it reached the desired curvature. Because the purest rock crystal was rare, the finest raw materials often had to be imported from distant, rugged regions through extensive trade networks. It was not until the Roman era that glassmaking techniques improved enough to create transparent glass lenses, though these early glass variants were still heavily plagued by bubbles and structural impurities that distorted the passage of light.

One should also add that it was not entirely unknown in the first millennium BCE for learned individuals to resort to the practical application of optical manipulation, particularly regarding heat. A well-known application of early convex lenses was their use as “burning glasses.” By focusing the sun’s rays onto a single, concentrated point, these lenses could easily ignite dry tinder. The ancient Greek playwright Aristophanes explicitly mentions a burning glass in his comedy The Clouds, written in 424 BCE, where a character plots to use the focused sunlight to melt the wax on a legal document and erase the writing. Furthermore, the Roman philosopher Pliny the Elder recorded that Emperor Nero, who was famously near-sighted, would watch the violent gladiator games in the Colosseum through a specially cut emerald, which likely acted to correct his vision or reduce the harsh glare of the Mediterranean sun.

While the Greeks and Romans made practical use of lenses, the theoretical understanding of how light actually worked remained deeply flawed for centuries. Many ancient thinkers, including the influential astronomer Ptolemy, subscribed to the ‘emission’ theory of vision, which incorrectly stated that the human eye emitted invisible rays of light that scanned objects in the environment. It was not until the Islamic Golden Age that this misconception was finally overturned. In the 11th century, the pioneering Arab mathematician and astronomer Ibn al-Haytham (often known in the West as Alhazen) published his monumental Book of Optics. Through rigorous, repeatable experiments, he proved the ‘intromission’ theory—that vision occurs because rays of light bounce off objects and travel into the eye. His book contained detailed mathematical diagrams of light refraction and the anatomy of the eye. When translated into Latin, his work fundamentally shifted the scientific landscape in Europe and laid the groundwork for future optical breakthroughs.

The practical application of Ibn al-Haytham’s theories began to surface in the monasteries of 13th-century Europe. As monks aged, their eyesight naturally deteriorated, threatening their ability to illuminate manuscripts and read sacred texts. To combat this, they developed ‘reading stones’— hemispherical segments of glass that, when laid flat against a page of text, magnified the letters beneath them. By the end of the 13th century, an unknown inventor in northern Italy took a monumental leap forward by placing two small magnifying lenses into frames that could balance on the bridge of the nose, creating the world’s first wearable spectacles. This liberalisation of vision correction had profound economic and social impacts. Suddenly, the working life of skilled craftsmen, tailors, and scholars was extended by decades, no longer curtailed by the natural onset of presbyopia.

The transition from simple reading aids to instruments of profound scientific discovery occurred dramatically at the dawn of the 17th century. While spectacles helped people see the world directly in front of them, the invention of the telescope and the microscope allowed humanity to see the previously invisible. In 1609, the Italian astronomer Galileo Galilei refined earlier Dutch designs to create a telescope capable of magnifying objects twenty times. When he pointed this optical device at the night sky, he discovered the craters of the moon and the orbiting satellites of Jupiter. Almost simultaneously, the microscope was developed. Later in the same century, the Dutch draper Antonie van Leeuwenhoek taught himself to grind extraordinarily precise spherical lenses. Though tiny, these lenses provided unprecedented magnification, allowing him to become the first human to observe single-celled organisms, which he famously dubbed ‘animalcules’.

Despite these incredible advances, 17th-century lenses suffered from a major flaw: chromatic aberration. Because a standard curved lens bends different colours of light at slightly different angles, early telescopes produced images surrounded by a distracting, blurry halo of rainbow colours. The renowned physicist Isaac Newton believed this problem was inherent to glass and could never be fixed, leading him to invent the reflecting telescope, which used curved mirrors instead of lenses to gather light. However, in the 1750s, the English optician John Dollond proved Newton wrong. Dollond successfully patented and commercialised the ‘achromatic lens’, which perfectly sandwiched together two different types of glass—crown glass and flint glass. The differing refractive properties of the two glass types cancelled out the colour distortion. This vital correction removed the final barrier to precision optics, cementing the foundation for the high-powered camera lenses and space telescopes we rely on today.
