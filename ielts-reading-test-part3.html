The hidden cost of urban noise

Dr. Aris Thorne looks at how human brains process background sound and discusses some of the challenges of battling widespread acoustic pollution.

Urban noise—both deliberately generated and accidentally produced—is perhaps an inevitable part of the densely populated world in which we live, but it is not a new problem. People likely have complained about the clamor of city life for roughly as long as urban settlements have existed. Generating loud sounds can offer an apparent opportunity to gain commercial advantage, to motivate workers to action, or even to establish territorial boundaries. Moreover, humans inadvertently have been subjecting one another to stressful acoustic environments for thousands of years, from the clatter of horse-drawn carriages to the shouts of street vendors.

However, we currently live in an era in which industrialization enables noise to reach massive populations distributed across sprawling metropolitan areas, and thus the potential for chronic and widespread effects from acoustic pollution now looms larger than in the past. Yet the means to mitigate urban noise might, over time, be found in those same patterns of technological advancement and of the facilitated development of smarter city infrastructures.

The main worry regarding environmental noise is its potential to unduly influence physiological and cognitive states, leading people to react and behave differently than they would if they were residing in quietude, as suggested by the research teams of Arline Bronzaft of the City University of New York and Stephen Stansfeld of Queen Mary University, among others. In other words, we worry that chronic noise might lead people to suffer from elevated stress hormones and that these biological shifts, especially when they occur among large groups of people, may have detrimental, downstream consequences for cardiovascular health, social harmony, and educational attainment.

At least three observations related to acoustic pollution in the contemporary urban environment warrant the attention of researchers, policy makers, and really everyone who commutes, works in open-plan offices, or lives near transportation hubs. First of all, people who encounter constant background noise tend to habituate to it, at least superficially. Secondly, local and national governments often do not block many types of industrial noise before it permeates spaces available to large populations. Thirdly, reducing noise pollution once it has become a structural norm can be a resource-intensive effort.

Knowing what happens when people initially encounter persistent noise holds tremendous importance for estimating the potential for subsequent health problems. Although it is fairly routine for individuals to come across acoustic disturbances, the question of exactly how—and when—we mentally process and filter out intrusive sounds has garnered intense neurobiological debate. The dilemma is neatly summarized by a contrast between how the 20th-century psychologists Donald Broadbent and Anne Treisman described human auditory attention, with conflicting predictions that only recently have been empirically tested in robust ways. Broadbent argued that a person only processes the meaning of a sound if they consciously focus on it, blocking out background noise entirely at an early stage; Treisman argued that people absorb all encountered auditory information by default, though attenuated, and then subsequently process or discard it through a separate cognitive filter. In recent decades, empirical evidence from the research teams of Nina Kraus at Northwestern University and Sophie Scott at University College London, among others, has supported Treisman’s account: people appear to encode all background noise as if it were relevant, even if only momentarily, and later tag the acoustic data as being either useful or irrelevant, a pattern that seems consistent with the observation that mental resources for deep processing physically reside in a different part of the brain than the resources used in initial auditory encoding.

What about our second observation that noise pollution often can appear in urban environments without being preemptively blocked? In support of this, one might consider the nature of regulatory structures in many Western nations: regulatory agencies here tend to focus on post hoc detection of acoustic violations. Organizations such as the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) offer considerable monitoring and notification functions, but these roles typically do not involve preemptive silencing. The EPA oversees acceptable decibel levels for construction equipment, for example, and has developed mechanisms through which citizens can report building sites in apparent violation of local noise ordinances. Such programs, although laudable and useful, do not keep the disruptive sounds out of residential neighborhoods in the first place. In addition, even noise that is successfully reduced can continue to affect stress levels for hours afterward.

This leads us to our third observation: a campaign to mitigate noise pollution, even if technologically sound, requires resources and planning to accomplish necessary reach and frequency of enforcement. For mitigation campaigns to be successful, communities need to be able to engage with them, which requires either effort to design interventions in ways that are accessible or effort to educate and sensitize populations to the invisible dangers of noise. That some communities might be unaware of the potential for cardiovascular harm also suggests the utility of public health literacy efforts as early as elementary school. Even with scientists pointing to the phenomenon of ‘noise fatigue’, people do not always distinguish between harmless city buzz and toxic decibel levels when scanning and processing their environments.

We live at a time when widespread acoustic pollution is common. Yet at this time many people also are passionately developing potential solutions and remedies. The journey forward undoubtedly will be a long and arduous one. Future remedies will require not only continued theoretical consideration but also the development and maintenance of consistent monitoring tools and a recognition among fellow members of society that infrastructure projects which find prominence in the city that are insufficiently based in acoustic design should be countered. Noise pollution arises as a result of human industry and human transportation needs. To overcome the worst effects of the phenomenon, we will need coordinated efforts over time, rather than any singular one-time panacea we could hope to offer.
