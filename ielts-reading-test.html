The Evolution of the Marathon Super Shoe

In October 2019, the Kenyan distance runner Eliud Kipchoge achieved what many scientists and sporting experts had long deemed biologically impossible: he ran a marathon distance in under two hours. It was a monumental achievement by any standard, made even more remarkable by the fact that he did this during an era when the marathon was already seeing unprecedentedly fast times. Kipchoge had already established himself as a dominant force, competing against formidable rivals such as Kenenisa Bekele and Wilson Kipsang, to name just a few. Yet, just a few years earlier, breaking the two-hour barrier was largely regarded as a fantasy, a distant milestone that might not be reached for another fifty years, if ever.

Of the various factors that account for this sudden leap in human performance, one technological shift was highly visible and intensely debated: the introduction of the carbon-plated "super shoe." In 2017, Kipchoge began working closely with his sponsor's innovation team—a collaboration that had a profound and visible impact on his stride and energy return. Another change within the sport, however, was much more subtle and related to how individual athletes interacted with this new technology. Like many traditional runners, Kipchoge had long preferred a minimalist racing flat that offered close contact with the ground. While he eventually embraced the thick foam of the new shoes, he and his team spent months secretly tweaking the exact curvature of the internal carbon plate. A minor adjustment, perhaps, but its significance in maintaining his late-race momentum should not be underestimated.

The modifications that elite runners make are just one of a number of options available to athletes looking to tweak their footwear in order to perfect their biomechanics. ‘Touring professionals have their shoes completely customised to their specific anatomical needs,’ says Dr. Sarah Jenkins, a UK-based sports biomechanist. ‘It’s a highly important part of performance maximisation.’ Consequently, the specific shoes worn by the world’s elite marathoners are not actually readily available to the public; rather, each shoe is individually manufactured to suit the specific foot strike of the runner who wears it. Take the Kenyan world record holder Brigid Kosgei, for example: ‘We’re very particular with our shoe specifications,’ her coaching team noted. ‘All her race shoes are sent from the manufacturer directly to our training camp in Kaptagat, where the foams go through a thorough customisation process.’ They explain how they have adjusted not only the shoe's heel-to-toe drop, but even experimented with different kinds of stiffness in the carbon plate. The shoes she uses now actually weigh less than the standard retail model and feature a customized traction pattern on the outsole.

The primary reason for these highly secretive modifications is simple: as the line between winning and losing a major marathon becomes thinner and thinner, even these slight changes become more and more important. A one percent increase in running economy can translate to a two-minute advantage over 26.2 miles. As a result, athletes and their sponsors are becoming increasingly creative with the modifications to their footwear as they look to maximise their competitive advantage.

Shoe modifications in track and field mainly date back to the late 1960s, when sprinters started competing with the so-called brush spike. It featured a sole covered in dozens of tiny, sharp needles that generated so much grip on the newly introduced synthetic tracks that it was quickly banned by the international governing body for athletics. However, within a decade or two, less extreme footwear modification became a regularity. Today it is, in many ways, an aspect of the sport that is equal in significance to nutrition or altitude training.

Modifications to modern running shoes can be divided into two primary categories: those to the midsole complex and those to the shoe’s upper. The former is far more common than the latter: the choice of the foam density and the tension of the carbon plate is something that nearly all professional runners experiment with. They will continually change it depending on various factors including the road surface, climatic conditions, and personal running styles. Some will even request last-minute alterations depending on how their legs feel during their final taper weeks.

At one time, all elite marathon shoes were constructed with standard EVA (ethylene-vinyl acetate) foam, which provided basic cushioning but little energy return. This all changed with the development of modern synthetic foams, specifically PEBA (polyether block amide), which are lighter and far more resilient. They are highly responsive, returning up to 85% of the energy compressed into them. Even so, many professional athletes continue to use a ‘hybrid set-up’, where a combination of both ultra-soft PEBA and firmer, traditional foams are layered to provide stability alongside speed.

Of the new materials, PEBA is by far the most widely used. It’s a perfect fit for the style of marathon racing now seen, where athletes try to sustain an aggressive, forward-leaning posture for two hours. Studies indicate that the energy return from a PEBA midsole is significantly greater than that from traditional EVA or other polyurethanes. In a sense, the development of PEBA foams has revolutionised the sport.

However, many athletes go beyond these basic adjustments to the foam and make highly personalized changes to the shoe’s upper. For example, much of the comfort of American professional runner Galen Rupp during hot races was attributed to the addition of custom-cut ventilation holes in his shoes, and today many professionals have the upper's breathability adjusted during the manufacturing process.

Other changes to the upper involve the fastening system. Runners have individual preferences for the tension across the top of the foot, and some will have the upper of one shoe bonded onto the sole of a completely different model. Other players make different changes. The professional elite runner Yuki Kawauchi completely replaced the original lacing of his marathon shoes with something much more elastic because the factory laces had previously felt restrictive to his midfoot.

Footwear customisation has pushed the standards of marathon running to greater levels that few could have anticipated in the days of thin, flat rubber soles, and it’s exciting to see what further developments there will be in the future.
