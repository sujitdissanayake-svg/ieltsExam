The development of the London underground railway

During the first half of the nineteenth century, London's population expanded at an extraordinary pace, and the central districts grew increasingly congested. Moreover, the rapid development of the overground railway network brought a rising influx of passengers into the capital. Yet, in 1846, a Royal Commission decreed that the railways should not penetrate the City, the historic and commercial heart of London. Consequently, the overground railway stations were established in a ring encircling the City. Within this perimeter lay densely packed, poorly constructed slums, while the streets teemed with horse drawn vehicles. Traversing the City became a formidable ordeal: a journey of merely eight kilometres by carriage or omnibus could consume an hour and a half. Numerous schemes were advanced to alleviate these difficulties, but few achieved success.

Among the most ardent proponents of a remedy for London's traffic difficulties was Charles Pearson, who served as a solicitor to the City of London. He perceived both social and economic benefits in constructing an underground railway that would interconnect the overground stations while simultaneously facilitating the clearance of London's slums. His vision was to resettle impoverished workers from the inner city slums into newly developed suburban districts, and to provide them with inexpensive rail transport to commute to their places of employment. Pearson's proposals attracted the support of certain businessmen, and in 1851 he presented a scheme to Parliament. Although it was rejected, it coincided with a separate proposal from another group for an underground connecting line, which Parliament approved.

The two groups united and founded the Metropolitan Railway Company in August 1854. The company's objective was to construct an underground railway extending from the Great Western Railway's (GWR) station at Paddington to the boundary of the City at Farringdon Street-a distance of nearly five kilometres. The organisation encountered considerable difficulty in securing the necessary capital for such a bold and costly enterprise, not least because of the critical articles published in the press. Opponents contended that the tunnels would collapse beneath the weight of traffic above, that buildings would be destabilised, and that passengers would be poisoned by the emissions from the locomotive engines. Nevertheless, Pearson and his associates remained steadfast in their determination.

The Great Western Railway (GWR), recognising that the new line would at last permit them to operate trains directly into the heart of the City, invested nearly £250,000 in the enterprise. Ultimately, over the course of five years, a total of £1 million was secured. The selected route passed beneath existing principal roads in order to reduce the expense of demolishing buildings. Although originally scheduled for completion within twenty one months, the construction of the underground line required three years. It was built just below street level by means of a technique known as "cut and cover." A trench approximately ten metres wide and six metres deep was excavated, its sides temporarily supported with timber beams. Brick walls were then erected, followed by the addition of a brick arch to form the tunnel. Finally, a two metre deep layer of soil was laid over the tunnel and the roadway above reconstructed.

The Metropolitan line, inaugurated on 10 January 1863, was the world's first subterranean railway. On its opening day, nearly 40,000 passengers travelled between Paddington and Farringdon, completing the journey in approximately eighteen minutes. By the close of its first year of operation, the Metropolitan had conveyed 9.5 million passengers. Even as services commenced, authorisation was granted for the line's initial extensions; these were constructed over the following five years, reaching Moorgate in London's east and Hammersmith in the west. The original intention had been to haul the trains with steam locomotives employing firebricks in their boilers to generate steam, yet these engines were never introduced. Instead, specially designed locomotives were utilised, equipped with water tanks in which steam could be condensed. Nevertheless, smoke and fumes persisted as a difficulty, despite the addition of ventilation shafts to the tunnels.

Despite the expansion of the underground railway, by the 1880s congestion on London's streets had intensified. The difficulty lay partly in the fact that the existing underground lines formed a circuit around the centre of London and extended outward to the suburbs, yet they did not traverse the capital's core. In this central district, the "cut and cover" method of construction was impracticable. The sole alternative was to drive tunnels deep beneath the ground.

Although the technology to construct such tunnels already existed, steam locomotives could not be employed in so confined an environment. Only with the advent of a dependable electric motor, together with a system for transmitting power from a generator to a moving train, did the world's first deep level electric railway-the City & South London-become feasible. The line opened in 1890, running from the City to Stockwell, south of the River Thames. The trains comprised three carriages propelled by electric engines. The carriages were narrow and fitted with diminutive windows positioned just below the roof, as it was assumed that passengers would have no desire to gaze upon the tunnel walls. The line was not without difficulties, most of which stemmed from an unreliable power supply. Although the City & South London Railway represented a remarkable technical achievement, it failed to yield a profit. In 1900, however, the Central London Railway-popularly known as the "Tuppenny Tube"-commenced operation with newly designed electric locomotives. It proved highly successful, and soon thereafter additional railways and extensions were incorporated into the expanding tube network. By 1907, the nucleus of today's Underground system was firmly established.
