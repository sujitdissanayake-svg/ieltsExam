Stadiums: past, present and future

Stadiums represent some of the earliest and most enduring forms of urban architecture. Expansive arenas designed for public gatherings and athletic contests stood at the heart of Western civic life as far back as the ancient Greek and Roman Empires, predating the construction of the great medieval cathedrals and the monumental railway stations of the 19th and 20th centuries that later came to dominate urban skylines. In contemporary times, however, stadiums are increasingly viewed with scepticism. Their construction costs often exceed £1 billion, and many venues built for global spectacles such as the Olympic Games or the FIFA World Cup have subsequently fallen into neglect and disrepair. Yet this outcome is not inevitable. Historical precedent demonstrates that stadiums can serve as catalysts for urban development and evolve in response to the cultural demands of each era. Even today, architects and planners are devising innovative strategies to reimagine the mono-functional sports arenas that became defining symbols of modernisation in the 20th century.

The amphitheatre of Arles, located in southwest France and accommodating 25,000 spectators, stands as one of the finest illustrations of the adaptability of stadium architecture. Constructed by the Romans in 90 AD, it was repurposed into a fortress with four towers following the fifth century, and later evolved into a village comprising more than 200 houses. With the rise of conservation movements in the 19th century, the structure was restored to its original function as an arena, hosting bullfights and thereby reclaiming its role as a venue for public spectacles. Equally notable is the grand arena of Verona in northern Italy, which could seat 30,000 spectators. Built 60 years prior to the Arles amphitheatre and 40 years before Rome's renowned Colosseum, it has withstood the passage of centuries and today is celebrated as one of the world's foremost opera venues, distinguished by its exceptional acoustics.

The central area of the Italian town of Lucca, known as the Piazza dell’Anfiteatro, offers another striking example of an amphitheatre being seamlessly integrated into the urban fabric. Much like the evolution of Arles, the site was gradually filled with structures during the Middle Ages and, by the 19th century, had served diverse purposes including housing, a salt depot, and a prison. Unlike Arles, however, it did not revert to its original function as an arena. Instead, it was transformed into a market square, designed by the Romanticist architect Lorenzo Nottolini. Today, remnants of the ancient amphitheatre remain embedded within the shops and residences that encircle the public square, preserving its historical essence within a modern civic setting.

Modern stadiums share many characteristics with the ancient amphitheatres originally designed for games. However, much of their inherent flexibility was diminished in the early 20th century, when new construction materials such as steel and reinforced concrete were introduced, and bright artificial lighting enabled matches to be staged at night. A significant number of these stadiums were built in suburban locations, intended solely for sporting purposes and typically surrounded by extensive parking facilities. As a result, they are often less accessible to the wider public, demand higher levels of energy consumption, and contribute to the intensification of urban heat.

Many of today’s most forward thinking architects envision the stadium as a transformative element within the urban landscape. Two strategies in particular have achieved notable success: positioning the stadium as an urban hub and reimagining it as a power generating facility. An increasing number of stadiums are being designed to incorporate public spaces and services that extend far beyond sport, including hotels, retail outlets, conference centres, restaurants and bars, children’s play areas, and landscaped green zones. Such mixed use developments reinforce compactness and multifunctionality, ensuring more efficient land use while simultaneously contributing to the regeneration of urban environments. By integrating these diverse facilities, stadiums open their doors to families and a broader cross section of society, rather than serving exclusively athletes and supporters. The United Kingdom offers several prominent examples: the mixed use complexes at Wembley and Old Trafford have established a model that has since been emulated by numerous stadiums worldwide.

The concept of stadiums functioning as power stations has emerged from the vision that energy challenges can be addressed by integrating interconnected buildings through a smart grid—an electricity supply network that employs digital communication technologies to detect and respond to local fluctuations in demand while minimizing energy losses. Stadiums are particularly well suited to this role, as their expansive canopies provide ample surface area for photovoltaic panels and rise to heights exceeding 40 metres, making them ideal for the installation of micro wind turbines. The Freiburg Mage Solar Stadium in Germany marked the beginning of this new generation of energy producing stadiums, followed by notable examples such as the Amsterdam Arena and the Kaohsiung Stadium. The latter, inaugurated in 2009, is equipped with 8,844 photovoltaic panels capable of generating up to 1.14 GWh of electricity annually. This innovation reduces carbon dioxide emissions by 660 tons each year and supplies as much as 80 percent of the surrounding area's energy needs when the stadium is not in use. Such achievements demonstrate that stadiums can actively serve their cities, delivering a measurable and positive impact in terms of reducing CO₂ emissions.

Sporting arenas have consistently occupied a central role in the cultural and civic life of cities. Across different eras, the stadium has acquired new significance and diverse functions: evolving from military fortress to residential settlement, from public gathering space to theatre, and most recently into a site for experimentation in advanced engineering. The contemporary stadium now integrates multiple purposes within a single structure, thereby contributing to the creation of sustainable urban futures.
