To Catch a King

Anna Keay reviews Charles Spencer's account of the pursuit of King Charles II during the English Civil War in the seventeenth century.

Charles Spencer's latest work, To Catch a King, recounts the dramatic hunt for King Charles II in the six weeks following his decisive defeat at the Battle of Worcester in September 1651. It is a compelling narrative. After the execution of his father by the Parliamentarians in 1649, the young Charles II compromised one of the very principles for which his father had died, striking an agreement with the Scots. In doing so, he accepted Presbyterianism as the national religion in exchange for being crowned King of Scots. His arrival in Edinburgh provoked a pre-emptive invasion of Scotland by the English Parliamentary army, which was subsequently followed by a Scottish incursion into England.

The opposing forces ultimately confronted one another at Worcester in western England in 1651. Following a decisive defeat on the meadows outside the city at the hands of the Parliamentarian army, the 21-year-old king became the target of a nationwide manhunt, with a substantial reward offered for his capture. Over the ensuing six weeks, he succeeded-through a succession of perilously close escapes-in evading his pursuers before eventually securing refuge in France. For the next nine years, the impoverished and dispossessed Charles wandered across Europe, accompanied only by a small circle of steadfast supporters.

Years later, following his restoration to the throne, the 50-year-old Charles II requested an audience with the writer and diarist Samuel Pepys. His purpose in asking Pepys to record the account was to ensure that this extraordinary episode would never fade from memory. Over the course of two extended sittings, each lasting three hours, the king recounted in meticulous detail his personal recollections of the six weeks he had spent as a fugitive. As the monarch and his secretary settled into conversation-a scene that seems tailor-made for a future dramatist-Charles began his narrative: "After the battle was so absolutely lost as to be beyond hope of recovery, I began to think of the best way of saving myself."

One of the great strengths of Spencer's book-derived in large part from his use of Charles II's own testimony alongside that of his loyal supporters-is the immediacy with which the reader is drawn into the unfolding drama. The day-by-day account of the king's flight offers vivid and captivating details: the cutting of his long hair with agricultural shears, the staining of his pale complexion with walnut leaves, and the extraordinary day he spent concealed on a branch of the great oak in Boscobel Wood while Parliamentary soldiers searched the forest floor beneath him. Spencer skilfully balances humour-such as the absurd refusal of Charles's companion Henry Wilmot to adopt a disguise, insisting it was beneath his dignity-with the palpable tension of moments when the king's presence was cautiously disclosed to trusted allies.

Charles's adventures following his defeat at the Battle of Worcester conceal an uncomfortable reality: although the execution of his father had horrified much of England, the arrival of his son at the head of a Scottish army was met not with welcome but with firmly barred doors. This reaction stemmed partly from the perception that he commanded what resembled a foreign invasion force, and partly from the widespread desire-after nearly a decade of civil war-to avoid renewed conflict. All the more striking, therefore, is the fact that Charles II himself cherished the tale for the rest of his life. He retold it endlessly, to the point of weary eye-rolling among courtiers, and initiated several measures to memorialise it. These included the creation of a new order of chivalry, the Knights of the Royal Oak, and the commissioning of vast oil paintings depicting his escape-among them a two-metre-wide canvas of Boscobel Wood and six monumental works portraying the king in flight. In 1660, Charles II further instructed the artist John Michael Wright to adorn the ceiling of his bedchamber with a vision of cherubs carrying an oak tree heavenward. Few monarchs have ever celebrated the lowest point of their lives with such enthusiasm-or indeed managed to survive such an extraordinary escape in the first place.

Charles Spencer proves an ideal figure to convey this remarkable story to a new generation. His brisk, engaging prose avoids modern idioms and instead elegantly animates the details of this extraordinary tale. He writes with balanced sympathy, acknowledging both the fugitive king and the determined republican regime that pursued him, and succeeds in exploring far more of the historical background than earlier works on the subject. Indeed, the opening third of To Catch a King is devoted to explaining how Charles II came to be at Worcester in the first place-a focus that, for many readers, will be reason enough to engage with the book.

The lingering question, ultimately, is what significance these events truly held. Would Charles II have been a different monarch had those six weeks of peril never occurred? The days and nights spent in concealment must surely have left their mark. Did the necessity of adopting disguises, surviving on wit and charm alone, and employing trickery and subterfuge to escape danger help shape his character? This is the one aspect where Spencer's book falls short. Its portrayal of Charles II in his later years as an ineffectual, pleasure-seeking ruler neither does justice to the man nor accurately reflects the complexity of his nature. Yet aside from this single shortcoming, To Catch a King remains an outstanding work, and readers approaching the tale with little prior knowledge will find themselves richly rewarded.
